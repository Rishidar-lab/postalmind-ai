Before I Tell the Story of Who I Loved

An opening note on memory, privacy, and the difference between remembering a person and claiming them.

A note on method. This is the introduction to a series about my own love
history. It is an account, not a verdict, and certainly not the last word on
anyone else. Where another person appears, they appear under a single-letter
pseudonym or a role, never a name. Where a detail would identify someone or
expose something that is theirs to disclose, it is left out or marked
redacted for privacy. Several specific facts in the later entries are
marked author review required before publication, and they will not be
published until I have checked them against what I actually remember and
against what is fair to say.

I have wanted to write this for a while and kept not doing it, which is usually a sign that the thing is worth being careful about. The hesitation was not about whether the experiences mattered. It was about the second person in every one of them. You cannot write honestly about loving someone without writing, at least a little, about them — their choices, their words, the way they changed a room. And they did not agree to be written about. Knowing someone, even loving them, does not grant you narrative rights over their life.

So the first entry in this series is not a story. It is the set of rules I am writing the rest under, and the reasons for them.

Whose account this is

Everything here is mine: my memory, my interpretation, my present-day reflection on what happened and what it did to me. That sounds like a disclaimer, and it is, but it is also the actual epistemic situation. I do not have access to the other person's experience of the same events. I have my side of a conversation and my reconstruction of theirs. When I write that someone felt something, I am inferring, and I will try to mark the inference as an inference.

This matters because the natural grammar of a personal essay is confident. "She thought." "He decided." "We both knew." That grammar smuggles in a claim to omniscience that no one writing about a relationship actually has. I would rather the prose be a little more awkward and a little more honest: I remember it this way; I read it as meaning this; I could be wrong about the second part and possibly the first.

Memory is not a recording

The other reason for caution is that memory does not retrieve the past so much as rebuild it, every time, using present materials. I know this in the abstract and I have watched it happen to me in particular. A scene I was sure of turns out, on reflection, to be a composite. A line I could have sworn was said verbatim is really the gist, polished by retelling. The emotional colour of a period shifts depending on how the story ended.

To remember a person is to keep rebuilding them from parts you still have. The parts are real. The person you assemble from them is a version, and the version is yours, not theirs.

This is why the series uses an editorial legend rather than a single narrative voice. Each substantial claim is tagged with how I know it:

  • Remembered event — something I directly experienced and recall, with the

ordinary unreliability of recall.

  • Contemporary record — something supported by a message, a note, a

calendar entry, or another artefact made at the time.

  • Present interpretation — my current reading of what something meant. This

is analysis, and it postdates the events, sometimes by years.

  • Uncertain / reconstructed memory — something I believe happened roughly

this way but cannot separate from later retelling.

  • Redacted for privacy — a real detail deliberately withheld because it

belongs to someone else.

The point of the legend is not academic. It is to stop me from doing the thing memoir does by default, which is to present a reconstruction with the confidence of a transcript.

The difference between remembering someone and claiming them

There is a specific failure I want to avoid. When a relationship ends, especially when it ends against your wishes, there is a strong pull toward telling the story in a way that settles it — that makes the other person legible, assigns the roles, and closes the account. It feels like grief work. Often it is really a bid for ownership: if I can fix the meaning of what happened, then in some sense I still hold it.

I do not want to do that here. Part of respecting someone after the fact is leaving their story open — not writing the sentence that explains them, because that sentence would be mine and not true. It is possible to have loved someone seriously, to have thought about them for years, and still not be entitled to define who they were. Those are separate things. Naming a feeling you had does not confer authority over the person who occasioned it.

What I am trying to understand

If the series is not an attempt to settle accounts, what is it for?

Mostly, I want to understand how attachment has shaped me — the actual mechanics of it, not the highlight reel. I have a particular way of coming to care about someone: slowly, and through the mind first. Attraction for me is not immediate and it is not primarily physical at the outset; it tends to arrive only after a real intellectual and emotional connection has formed, and then it arrives completely. I have spent a long time without good language for that, and without knowing whether it made me unusual in a way that mattered. It does have consequences — for how I read early interest, for what I expect from relationships, for how I handle the gap between what I feel privately and how any of it reads socially.

There are also questions the series keeps circling that I have not resolved:

  • What does honesty actually require you to disclose to a future partner about

a past attachment — and what is it fairer to keep?

  • What does it mean for love to remain meaningful after the relationship

itself has become impossible? Is that fidelity, or is it just an unwillingness to let go?

  • How much of the pressure around relationships — the expectation of a

particular kind of marriage, on a particular timeline, with the approval of particular people — is worth resisting, and what does resisting it cost the people around you as well as you?

  • Where is the line between your own truth and someone else's privacy, when the

two are genuinely entangled?

I do not have clean answers. The series is the process of working on them, not the report of having finished.

The people, and how they will appear

There is essentially one sustained attachment at the centre of what follows. In the entries, that person is P. P is a real person; "P" is not their initial and is not meant to be decoded. Details that would identify P — where we met, what they do, their family circumstances, anything about their health, their relationships, or their private conduct — are either omitted or marked redacted. I am not going to reconstruct P's inner life, quote conversations I cannot verify, or present my interpretation of their motives as fact.

A few dates anchor the chronology. Each one is marked author review required before publication in the entry where it appears, because I want to check the exact dates against contemporary records and, more importantly, think about whether stating them publicly is fair before I do:

  • an approximate first meeting;
  • a point at which family opposition became explicit;
  • a requested closure conversation shortly after.

No other individuals are named or characterised. If a later entry needs a third person to make sense, they get a role ("a relative", "a mutual friend") and nothing more, and if even that risks identifying them, the passage waits.

The rules, stated plainly

For my own accountability, here is what I am committing to:

  1. No real names of third parties, and no details that function as names.
  2. No invented dialogue and no invented scenes. If I did not witness it or

record it, I do not dramatise it.

  1. No framing of allegations as facts, and no diagnosing anyone — including

myself — with anything.

  1. No disclosure of another person's health, sexuality, family history,

private messages, or intimate conduct without their explicit approval, which I do not currently have and may never seek.

  1. Interpretation is labelled as interpretation. Memory that has gone soft is

labelled as such.

  1. Anything marked author review required stays unpublished until I have done

that review.

  1. The series can be revised. Earlier layers stay visible; later corrections

are added on top, in keeping with how the rest of this archive works.

Why publish any of it

A fair question is why this belongs in public at all, if so much of it has to be withheld.

The honest answer is that the withholding is part of the subject. What you owe another person after love — in privacy, in restraint, in the stories you decline to tell — is one of the least examined parts of the whole experience. Most accounts of relationships are written as though the other person's privacy ended when the relationship did. I do not think it did. Working out, in public, how to write about something important while protecting someone who is no longer in a position to object seems worth doing, even if the result is more careful and less complete than a tell-all would be.

The other reason is smaller and more personal. I want to become accountable for my own version of events — to say clearly this is what I remember, this is what I made of it, this is where I know I am guessing — rather than carrying around an unexamined story that quietly hardens into fact. Writing it down under these constraints is a way of holding myself to the more honest version.

That is the whole of the introduction. The entries that follow are, for now, private drafts. They will stay that way until the review each one needs is actually done.
