Reading the human–AI literature

An annotated starting point for the research Regard leans on.

This note collects the literature families Regard's case studies draw on. It is a starting point, not a survey, and it is written by a non-specialist reader. Each entry says briefly what the work does and — as important — what it does not settle.

Anthropomorphism

Epley, Waytz & Cacioppo (2007) propose that we attribute human characteristics to non-human agents more readily when human-like explanations are cognitively available, when we are motivated to predict an agent's behaviour, and when we lack social connection. Useful for understanding why a fluent chatbot provokes social responses. It is a psychological theory of the human side; it says nothing about the machine's inner status.

The media equation and "computers as social actors"

Reeves & Nass (1996) and Nass & Moon (2000) report that people apply politeness norms, reciprocity and personality judgements to computers showing minimal responsive behaviour — while denying that they think the computer is a person. The effect is fast and hard to suppress. Caveat: much of the original work predates conversational systems, and some findings have been revisited since.

Parasocial interaction

Horton & Wohl (1956) coined para-social interaction for the one-sided sense of a relationship that television viewers develop with performers. The concept transfers imperfectly to AI — a chatbot does respond to you — but the core idea, that a felt relationship can form without mutuality, is directly relevant.

Cognitive offloading and the extended mind

Clark & Chalmers (1998) argue that reliably available, trusted external resources can count as part of a cognitive system. Risko & Gilbert (2016) review the empirical literature on offloading memory and computation to the environment. Hollan, Hutchins & Kirsh (2000) frame cognition as distributed across people and artefacts. Together these describe how a person plus their notes, repositories and assistants can form one problem-solving unit. The extended-mind thesis specifically remains a contested philosophical position, not an established scientific result.

Automation bias

Skitka, Mosier & Burdick (1999) show experimentally that people using a decision aid can make errors of omission and commission by deferring to it — checking a plausible automated result less than they would check their own work. This is the failure mode that "trust but verify" is meant to prevent.

The oldest case

Weizenbaum (1966) documents users confiding in ELIZA, a simple pattern-matcher, and attributing understanding to it. The distance between a system's mechanism and the response it provokes is not a new problem.
